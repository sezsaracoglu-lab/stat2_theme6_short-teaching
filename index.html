📜 The rules of the game

Ten rules that govern the whole method. Each step then has two or three of its own, behind the 📜 chip inside that step.

  1. One number per person, one grouping variable. The dependent variable is quantitative and measured on everybody; the factor is categorical and says which group you are in. If the outcome is a category, you need a different test, not ANOVA.
  2. Two groups is a t test. Three or more is ANOVA. And for exactly two groups the two agree: F = t² — for the pooled-variance, two-sided t test. ANOVA is the general method; the t test is its special case.
  3. The question is always about MEANS. The name says variance because of HOW it works, not what it asks.
  4. All variation is either BETWEEN or WITHIN. Systematic group differences, or individual differences. There is no third source, and the whole method is the comparison of those two.
  5. Everything splits, with no remainder. SS Total = SS Between + SS Within, and df Total = df Between + df Within. Both are free checks on your arithmetic.
  6. Divide ACROSS the row, compare DOWN the column. MS = SS ÷ df along one row. F = MS Between ÷ MS Within down the column.
  7. F is a signal-to-noise ratio. Around 1 means nothing special, and an F below 1 is perfectly normal. Only an F past the critical value is evidence.
  8. The alternative hypothesis has no direction. Hₐ is “at least one mean differs” — never “all differ”, never one-tailed. (The TEST is right-tailed because squaring removes signs; that is a different thing.)
  9. A significant F never names a pair. It says “not all equal”. Which groups differ is a separate question, answered only by post-hoc tests with a corrected alpha.
  10. Significance and size are different questions. F and p say whether it is real; R² says how much it matters. An exam usually wants both, and causality needs a third thing again: manipulation plus random assignment.

🎯 Learning objectives

  • Say what one-way ANOVA is for, and why three t tests are not an acceptable substitute.
  • Name the factor, its levels and the dependent variable, and produce k and N from a description.
  • Write H₀ and Hₐ in the fixed ANOVA form.
  • Explain the two sources of variation in plain words, and say which is signal and which is noise.
  • Compute SS Between, SS Within and SS Total, and check that they balance.
  • Produce all three degrees of freedom and both mean squares.
  • Compute F, report it with its pair of df, and say what F ≈ 1 would mean.
  • Turn F into a p range using a critical-value table, including when your df is not listed.
  • Compute and interpret R² as a percentage of explained variation.
  • Write the three separate sentences — calculation, decision, conclusion — and say when a causal claim is allowed.
  • Explain why post-hoc tests need a corrected alpha, and compute a Bonferroni alpha.
  • List the three assumptions that make a one-way ANOVA valid.

🏛️ The whole method in one paragraph

One-way ANOVA answers one question — are these group means different? — by taking all the variation in the scores and splitting it in two. Part of why people differ is which group they were in, and that is SS Between; the rest is that people simply differ, and that is SS Within. Those two totals are not comparable as they stand, because one is built from a handful of group means and the other from every score, so each is divided by its own degrees of freedom to become a mean square. Their ratio is F. If the null hypothesis were true, both mean squares would be estimating the same ordinary noise and F would land near 1, so an F far above 1 is evidence against it — and how far above depends on the two df, which is what the F table encodes. A significant F earns one modest sentence: in the population, the means are not all equal. It does not say which groups differ, how big the effect is, or why — those need post-hoc tests, R², and a design with manipulation and random assignment respectively. In the lecture’s own study the whole chain runs: 561.41 and 1883.96 split out of 2445.37, become 280.71 and 34.89 on 2 and 54 degrees of freedom, give F = 8.05 with p < .001, and an R² of .23 — a real effect, strong by this course’s standard, and causal because the sound was manipulated and assigned at random.

1step

🎯 Start with the question

What ANOVA is for — and why three t-tests will not do

⬇ builds onNothing yet. This is the ground floor.⬆ hands upA single research question: “are these group means different?”
  • ▸The research question never changes: ARE THE MEANS DIFFERENT?
  • ▸Two groups → an independent t test does the job perfectly.
  • ▸Three or more → one-way ANOVA, which tests them all in a single F test.
🪝 memory hookANOVA is the t test that learned to count past two.

In words

Everything begins with one sentence. You have one number measured on every person, and people come in several groups, and you want to know whether the groups differ on that number. You already know how to do this for TWO groups — that is the independent-samples t test. One-way ANOVA is the same question asked of three groups, or four, or ten.

  • The obvious alternative — a t test for every pair — quietly destroys your error rate.
  • Three pairs each 95% safe give 0.95 × 0.95 × 0.95 ≈ 86%, so about a 14% chance of a false alarm. (That product treats the tests as independent — the lecture’s own approximation.)

Rules of the game for step 1

  • One quantitative score per person. If the outcome is a category or a proportion, you need a different test, not ANOVA.
  • One grouping variable. That is what “one-way” means.
  • Ask the question before you look for a formula. The question picks the test, not the other way round.

Say it to a friend

“I want to compare the average score across several groups at once. A t test only handles two, and running it three times would make me far too likely to find something that isn’t there — so I use one ANOVA instead.”

Key insight

Nothing about the DATA changes when you move from a t test to an ANOVA. The dependent variable is still a number and the independent variable is still a category. Only the number of groups grows.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Running several t tests “just to see”. Each one carries its own 5% risk, and the risks accumulate — which is exactly the problem ANOVA was invented to solve.

🧪 Demo 1 — which test does it call for?
Pick one. You will get the reasoning either way.

Check yourself

Reaction time in milliseconds is compared for people who drank coffee, tea or water. Which test, and why?
One-way ANOVA. Reaction time is quantitative — the dependent variable — and drink type is a categorical factor with three levels. Three groups rules out the two-sample t test.
And if there had been only coffee and water?
Two groups, so an independent-samples t test. An ANOVA would also be valid and would give exactly the same answer: for two groups, F = t².

🏷️ Name the design

Factor, levels, dependent variable — and the two numbers k and N

⬇ builds onThe research question from step 1.⬆ hands upk (how many groups) and N (how many scores). Every degree of freedom comes from these.
  • ▸DEPENDENT VARIABLE — the score. Quantitative, continuous, one number per person.
  • ▸FACTOR — the independent variable. The categorical variable saying which group you are in.
  • ▸LEVELS — the categories of the factor. In the lecture’s study: Mozart, Michael Jackson, Weather report.
🪝 memory hookFind the measured number first. Everything that sorts people is a factor.

In words

Before any arithmetic, you have to be able to read the design off the page. Three words do all the work. Get them right and two numbers fall out, and those two numbers decide everything that follows.

  • k = the number of levels. Here k = 3.
  • N = the number of scores in total. Here N = 57 — people, not groups.

Rules of the game for step 2

  • The dependent variable is always the thing MEASURED. The factor is always the thing that GROUPS.
  • Count levels for k; count people for N. The commonest slip in the whole theme is using the group size where N belongs.
  • Between-subject design: each person is in exactly one group and gives exactly one score.

Say it to a friend

“The thing I measured is the score — that’s the dependent variable. The thing that splits people into groups is the factor, and its categories are the levels. Count the levels, count the people, and you’ve got everything you need.”

Key insight

Write “k = ___, N = ___” at the top of your working before you do anything else. Every df in this entire lecture comes out of those two numbers, and so does the critical value you look up.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Counting the factor as a level, or the levels as separate factors. One factor with three levels — not three factors. And the dependent variable is never a level.

Check yourself

In the Mozart study, what are the factor, the levels, k, the dependent variable and N?
Factor: sound. Levels: Mozart, Michael Jackson, Weather report, so k = 3. Dependent variable: spatial awareness score. N = 19 + 20 + 18 = 57.
A study compares social intelligence across Regular, Montessori, Jenaplan and Dalton schools, 25 pupils each. Give k and N.
k = 4 and N = 100. The group size, 25, is not N — it is what you multiply by k.

👀 Look at the data first

A picture, before any formula

⬇ builds onThe design from step 2 — you now know what is on each axis.⬆ hands upA rough expectation. If the picture shows nothing, a big F would be a surprise worth double-checking.
  • ▸SCATTERPLOT: the factor along the bottom, the score up the side, one dot per person.
  • ▸BOXPLOT: the same thing summarised — the middle, the spread, the outliers.
  • ▸What you are looking for: do the groups sit at different heights, and how much do they overlap?
🪝 memory hookDistance between the groups, overlap inside them. That is the whole picture.

In words

The lecture puts the graph before the arithmetic, and that order is deliberate. A scatterplot or a boxplot takes ten seconds and tells you what answer to expect — which means an arithmetic slip later has something to collide with.

  • In the lecture’s data, Mozart (20.74) and Michael Jackson (20.70) sit together and Weather (14.00) sits well below.
  • But the spread inside each group is large — variances of 28, 25 and 52 — and that overlap is what F has to overcome.

Rules of the game for step 3

  • Always look before you compute. The picture is evidence, not decoration.
  • A picture can SUGGEST a difference. It can never establish one — only the test does that.
  • Two things matter in the picture: how far apart the groups sit, and how spread out people are inside them. Those two become the two halves of F.

Say it to a friend

“Before I calculate anything I just plot it. If the three clouds of dots sit at obviously different heights and barely overlap, I already know roughly what the test will say.”

Key insight

The two things you notice in the picture are exactly the two things ANOVA measures: the distance between the group means becomes SS Between, and the overlap inside the groups becomes SS Within.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Skipping straight to the formula. Half of all arithmetic errors would be caught in two seconds by a glance at the plot you did not draw.

Check yourself

What would a plot look like if H₀ were clearly true?
Three clouds of dots sitting at roughly the same height, heavily overlapping — the group means almost on top of each other relative to the spread.
Your plot shows three well-separated groups but you compute F = 0.8. What should you do?
Check your arithmetic. A clearly separated picture and an F below 1 do not belong together; one of the two is wrong, and it is usually the sum of squares.

0️⃣ Write the hypotheses

Two sentences, and they never change shape

⬇ builds onThe names from step 2 — you need the levels to write H₀.⬆ hands upThe claim the whole test is about to put on trial.
  • ▸μ means the mean score in the POPULATION, not in your sample.
  • ▸H₀: μ(Mozart) = μ(Michael) = μ(Weather) — all the population means are equal.
  • ▸Hₐ: in the population, at least one mean is different from the others.
🪝 memory hookAll equal, or not all equal. That is the whole menu.

In words

A statistical test is an argument about a claim, so the claim has to be written down first. In ANOVA both hypotheses have a fixed form, which is good news: you can write them before you have seen a single number.

  • Either one is true or the other is. There is no third option.
  • Hₐ has no direction: there is no one-tailed one-way ANOVA.

Rules of the game for step 4

  • The hypotheses are about the POPULATION. Your sample means will differ a bit whatever is true.
  • Hₐ is always “at least one differs”, never “they all differ”.
  • You never conclude that H₀ is TRUE. You either reject it or you fail to reject it.

Say it to a friend

“The boring possibility is that the groups really are all the same and any difference I see is luck. The interesting one is that at least one of them is genuinely different. The test decides which is more believable.”

Key insight

Writing Hₐ as “at least one differs” is not pedantry — it is exactly what a significant F entitles you to say later, and getting it right here saves the mark at the end.

The mistake almost everyone makes

“Hₐ: all three means are different.” Too strong. One group sitting apart from two identical ones already makes H₀ false, and F cannot tell those two situations apart.

Check yourself

Write H₀ in words for a study comparing four teaching methods on exam score.
In the population, the mean exam score is the same for all four teaching methods.
Can you write a one-tailed Hₐ for a one-way ANOVA?
No. With three or more groups there is no single direction to point in. The F test is right-tailed, but that is a property of the test statistic, not a one-sided hypothesis.

🔀 Find the two sources of variation

Why a test about MEANS is called analysis of VARIANCE

⬇ builds onThe hypotheses from step 4 — you now know what you are trying to disprove.⬆ hands upThe idea the rest of the method rests on: all variation is either signal or noise.
  • ▸BETWEEN: systematic GROUP differences, caused by the independent variable. The signal.
  • ▸WITHIN: error, noise, INDIVIDUAL differences between subjects. Two people in the same group still differ.
  • ▸Every bit of variation in the scores is one or the other. There is no third source.
🪝 memory hookPart of it is the group you were in. Part of it is just that people differ.

In words

This is the conceptual heart of the lecture and the step people skip. Ask yourself: why do two people have different scores? There are exactly two possible reasons, and ANOVA is built entirely on separating them.

  • ANOVA = ANalysis Of VAriance — it compares means by looking at variances.
  • The three variances it looks at: of all the scores, of the scores within each group, and of the group means.

Rules of the game for step 5

  • Between is measured from the OVERALL mean. Within is measured from each group’s OWN mean. That single difference is what makes them different quantities.
  • Within is not a nuisance to be removed — it is the yardstick. Without it you cannot tell whether a gap is impressive.
  • The name is about the method, not the question. ANOVA compares MEANS.

Say it to a friend

“Some of the difference between two people is because they were in different groups — that’s the thing I care about. The rest is just people being people. The whole method is about measuring those two separately and comparing them.”

Key insight

If the null hypothesis is true, the groups are not really different, so the variation between the group means and the variation inside the groups are both measuring the same thing: noise. That is the pivot the entire test turns on.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Thinking “analysis of variance” means the question is about variances. It is not. The question is always about MEANS; variances are only how the answer is reached.

Check yourself

Two students in the Mozart group score 24 and 17. Which source is that?
Within. They are in the same group, so the factor cannot explain the gap — it is individual difference, noise.
The Weather group averages 14.00 and the Mozart group 20.74. Which source?
Between. That is a difference between group means, which is what the independent variable is supposed to produce.

➕ Measure them — the three sums of squares

The same sum three times, measured from three different places

⬇ builds onThe two sources from step 5 — now you put numbers on them.⬆ hands upSS Between, SS Within and SS Total.
  • ▸SS TOTAL = sum of (score − overall mean)². The variation in ALL the scores. In the lecture: 2445.37.
  • ▸SS BETWEEN = sum of groupsize × (group mean − overall mean)². The variation in the GROUP MEANS. Here: 561.41.
  • ▸SS WITHIN = sum of (score − its own group mean)². The variation INSIDE the groups. Here: 1883.96.
🪝 memory hookDistance from somewhere, squared, added up. Only the “somewhere” changes.

In words

A sum of squares answers “how much variation is there?”. You take each number, see how far it sits from a reference point, square that distance so the pluses and minuses cannot cancel, and add everything up. All three sums of squares are that same operation — only the reference point changes.

  • And the line that must always balance: SS Total = SS Between + SS Within.
  • Check: 561.41 + 1883.96 = 2445.37 ✓

Rules of the game for step 6

  • Square the differences — otherwise they cancel to exactly zero and tell you nothing.
  • Weight SS Between by the group size. A mean built from 20 people carries more evidence than one built from 5.
  • Given any two of the three sums of squares, the third is one subtraction away. Exams exploit this constantly.

Say it to a friend

“A sum of squares is just a total amount of spread. I measure how far things sit from a middle, square it so nothing cancels, and add it all up — once from the overall average, once from each group’s own average.”

Key insight

SS Between and SS Within are not two separate measurements of the data — they are the two halves the total splits into, with no remainder. That is why the balance check always works, and why it is the cheapest error-catcher in the theme.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Forgetting the group size in SS Between, and forgetting the squaring. Both produce a number that looks plausible and breaks the balance check — which is exactly why you do the check.

🧪 Demo — split the sums of squares yourself
Three group means, three group sizes, three group variances. The lecture’s own numbers are loaded.


Check yourself

SS Total = 400 and SS Within = 310. What is SS Between?
400 − 310 = 90. The three always add up, so any missing one is a subtraction.
Three groups of 10 have means 12, 15 and 18, and the overall mean is 15. Compute SS Between.
10(12−15)² + 10(15−15)² + 10(18−15)² = 90 + 0 + 90 = 180. Note the middle group contributes nothing — its mean sits exactly on the overall mean.

⚖️ Make them comparable

Degrees of freedom, and then mean squares

⬇ builds onThe three sums of squares from step 6, and k and N from step 2.⬆ hands upMS Between and MS Within — two numbers now on the same scale.
  • ▸df Between = k − 1 = 3 − 1 = 2. (You have k group means; once the overall mean is fixed, only k − 1 are free.)
  • ▸df Within = N − k = 57 − 3 = 54. (All N scores, minus one for each group mean you had to estimate.)
  • ▸df Total = N − 1 = 56, and it must equal the other two added together.
🪝 memory hookA sum keeps growing. An average does not. Averages can be compared.

In words

Here is the problem this step exists to solve. SS Between is built from only 3 group means; SS Within is built from all 57 scores. The second will be bigger almost automatically, so comparing them directly would be unfair. Dividing each by its own degrees of freedom puts them on one scale.

  • MS = SS ÷ df, always along the SAME row. MS Between = 561.41 ⁄ 2 = 280.71.
  • MS Within = 1883.96 ⁄ 54 = 34.89.
📌 A note on the lecture slides
The lecture prints MS Between twice. On the “Mean Squares” slide it reads 286.71, but 561.41 ⁄ 2 = 280.71, and 280.71 is what the F calculation and the final ANOVA table both use. The 286.71 is a typo — use 280.71.

Rules of the game for step 7

  • Divide ACROSS the row to get a mean square. Never across rows.
  • df Between + df Within must equal N − 1. If they do not, one of them is wrong.
  • A sum of squares grows with every person you add; a mean square does not. Only the average is comparable.

Say it to a friend

“The two totals aren’t fair to compare, because one is built from three numbers and the other from fifty-seven. So I turn each into an average amount of variation per piece of information, and then they’re on the same footing.”

Key insight

A mean square is an estimate of variance. If H₀ is true, MS Between and MS Within are both estimating the same ordinary noise — which is why their ratio should then be about 1. That is the next step, and this one makes it possible.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Taking df from the wrong row — dividing SS Between by df Within, for instance. Mean squares are computed along a row, never across the table.

🧪 Demo 2 — the degrees-of-freedom machine

Check yourself

A study has 5 groups and 45 people. Give all three df.
df Between = 5 − 1 = 4. df Within = 45 − 5 = 40. df Total = 44. Check: 4 + 40 = 44 ✓
Why is there no MS Total row in the table?
SS Total ÷ df Total is a real number — the overall variance of the scores — but F does not need it, so the cell is left empty.

🧮 Build the ratio

F — signal divided by noise

⬇ builds onThe two mean squares from step 7.⬆ hands upOne number, F, that carries the entire comparison.
  • ▸F = MS Between ÷ MS Within. In the lecture: 280.71 ⁄ 34.89 = 8.05.
  • ▸Think of it as (treatment effect + chance) ÷ chance.
  • ▸If H₀ is TRUE, the top and the bottom are both estimating the same noise, so F should land around 1.
🪝 memory hookF = signal ÷ noise. Near 1 means nothing. Well above 1 means something.

In words

Everything so far has been preparing two numbers. This step divides one by the other, and that single division is the whole test. Everything after it is interpretation.

  • An F below 1 is perfectly normal and still consistent with H₀ — it is not an error.
  • A large F says the group means are further apart than the within-group spread explains.

Rules of the game for step 8

  • F is always reported with BOTH degrees of freedom, numerator first: F(2, 54) = 8.05.
  • F can never be negative — both halves are built from squares.
  • A big F is not a big effect. F mixes the effect with the sample size and the noise; the size question comes at step 10.

Say it to a friend

“F asks whether the gaps between the groups are bigger than the gaps between people inside them. If the answer is ‘about the same’, F comes out near one and there’s nothing to see.”

Key insight

The same group means can give wildly different F values. Keep the means fixed and squeeze the within-group spread, and F shoots up; widen it, and F collapses. That is the whole reason the denominator is there.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Dividing SS Between by SS Within. F is a ratio of MEAN squares — you must convert both first, and that conversion is what the degrees of freedom are for.

🧪 Demo 3 — what F is actually comparing

Check yourself

MS Between = 20 and MS Within = 5. What is F, and what does it suggest?
F = 4.00. The variation between the group means is four times what individual differences alone would produce — promising, though whether it is significant depends on the df.
Someone reports F = −2.3. What has gone wrong?
Something has, certainly. F is a ratio of two quantities built from squares, so it cannot be negative.

📑 Judge it

The F distribution, the critical value, and p

⬇ builds onF from step 8, and both df from step 7.⬆ hands upA p-value — or, by hand, a range for one.
  • ▸There is not one F distribution but an infinite family, indexed by the two df — the same two you used for the mean squares.
  • ▸The p-value is the right-tail probability of getting an F this large or larger IF H₀ were true.
  • ▸The lecture’s df are (2, 54). The table has no row for 54, so it uses (2, 50) — the next SMALLER df. That gives a slightly larger critical value, so a rejection reached that way is safe.
🪝 memory hookRun along the row until your F stops winning. That bracket is your p.

In words

F = 8.05 means nothing on its own. Is 8.05 big? That depends entirely on how many groups and how many people you had — which is exactly what the two degrees of freedom carry. The F distribution is what H₀ predicts your F would look like, and you compare against it.

  • Critical values at (2, 50): 2.41 at p = .10, 3.18 at .05, 3.97 at .025, 5.06 at .01, 7.96 at .001.
  • Observed F = 8.05 beats even 7.96, so p < .001.

Rules of the game for step 9

  • Enter the table with the PAIR (df between, df within), numerator first.
  • Your df missing? Drop to the next smaller one. Fewer df means a larger critical value, so a rejection you reach that way would also hold on the correct row. (A near-miss is less clear-cut, so say which row you used.)
  • By hand, p comes as a RANGE. Report “p < .001”, never “p = .001”.

Say it to a friend

“Eight means nothing by itself. What matters is that with these group sizes, anything past about eight would happen less than one time in a thousand by luck — so eight is rare enough to believe.”

Key insight

The test is right-tailed even though Hₐ has no direction. Squaring destroys the signs, so all the evidence against H₀ piles into the upper tail — which is why there is only ever one critical value to look up.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Writing the df the wrong way round. F(2, 54) and F(54, 2) have completely different critical values, and only one of them is yours.

Check yourself

Your F is 3.50 on df (2, 50). Using the values above, what is your p range?
It beats 3.18 but not 3.97, so .025 < p < .05. Significant at α = .05, not at .025.
Why does using df = 50 instead of 54 not invalidate the conclusion?
Because 50 gives a slightly LARGER critical value than 54 would. If your F clears the stricter bar, it would also clear the correct one.

📐 Size it

R² — how much of the variation the factor explains

⬇ builds onSS Between and SS Total from step 6.⬆ hands upA proportion between 0 and 1 that answers a completely different question from p.
  • ▸R² (also written η², eta-squared) = SS Between ÷ SS TOTAL.
  • ▸In the lecture: 561.41 ⁄ 2445.37 = .23.
  • ▸Read it as a percentage of the variation in the dependent variable: 23%.
🪝 memory hookF asks “is it real?”. R² asks “does it matter?”.

In words

Step 9 told you the difference is probably real. It said nothing about whether it matters. Those are two separate questions and exams ask both, so you need a second number.

  • Say it in a full sentence: “Of all the variation in spatial awareness scores, 23% is explained by which sound people heard.”
  • In this course, R² above about .20 counts as a strong effect — so .23 is strong.

Rules of the game for step 10

  • The denominator is SS TOTAL, never SS Within.
  • R² runs from 0 to 1 and has no units.
  • Quote it WITH the dependent variable named. A bare “.23” leaves the interpretation mark on the table.

Say it to a friend

“The test told me the difference is probably real. This number tells me how much of what I actually saw it accounts for — about a quarter. Real and small is a perfectly possible answer.”

Key insight

Significance depends on sample size; effect size does not. With hundreds of people a trivial gap turns significant, which is why a second number is needed to say whether anyone should care.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reading a tiny p as a big effect. p = .0001 alongside R² = .02 means a real difference that explains almost nothing.

🧪 Demo 5 — significance and size are different questions

Check yourself

SS Between = 180 and SS Within = 420. What is R²?
SS Total = 600, so R² = 180 ⁄ 600 = .30 — 30% of the variation explained.
A study reports p < .001 and R² = .02. How do you describe it?
The difference is almost certainly real, but tiny: the factor explains only 2% of the variation. Almost certainly a very large sample.

🗣️ Say what it means

The decision, the conclusion, and whether you may claim a cause

⬇ builds onp from step 9 and R² from step 10.⬆ hands upThe sentences that actually earn the marks.
  • ▸THE CALCULATION: a number with its df. “F(2, 54) = 8.05.”
  • ▸THE DECISION: about H₀, carrying the statistic and p. “p < .001, so H₀ is rejected.” Never “H₀ is accepted”.
  • ▸THE CONCLUSION: about the POPULATION, in words, no symbols. “In the population, the three sounds do not all produce the same average spatial awareness score.”
🪝 memory hookStatistic → decision → conclusion. Three sentences, three marks.

In words

This is where the marks are, and where most of them are lost. There are three different sentences and examiners mark them separately — the calculation, the decision about the hypothesis, and the conclusion about the real world.

  • THE SIZE: “R² = .23, so the sound accounts for about 23% of the variation in the scores.”
  • CAUSALITY: allowed here, because the sound was manipulated and people were randomly assigned.

Rules of the game for step 11

  • If p > .05 you FAIL TO REJECT H₀. You never accept it, and you never conclude the means are equal.
  • Causality needs two things: the factor was MANIPULATED, and people were RANDOMLY ASSIGNED. Both, or neither.
  • The conclusion sentence must mention the population, or an equivalent. Without it the interpretation mark is gone.

Say it to a friend

“The music made a real difference to the scores, and it accounts for about a quarter of why people differed. And because we chose who heard what and assigned them at random, we can say the music caused it — not just that it goes with it.”

Key insight

A significant F says “not all equal”. It does not say which group is best, how big the gap is, or in which direction things run. Each of those needs a different piece of evidence.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Stopping at “p < .05, reject H₀”. That is the decision, not the conclusion — and the interpretation marks are all in the sentence that follows it.

🧪 Demo 6 — may you claim a cause?

Check yourself

A study compares social intelligence across four school types and finds p = .01. May you say school type causes the difference?
No. Nobody was randomly assigned to a school type — pupils arrived already in their groups. The schools differ, but this design cannot say why.
Write the full conclusion for the lecture’s study.
“A one-way ANOVA compared spatial awareness across three sounds (N = 57), F(2, 54) = 8.05, p < .001, R² = .23. H₀ is rejected: in the population the three sounds do not all produce the same average score, and the sound accounts for about 23% of the variation. Because sound was manipulated with random assignment, a causal claim is justified.”

🔎 Follow up and check

Which pairs differ — and the three assumptions

⬇ builds onA significant F from step 9, and MS Within from step 7.⬆ hands upA verdict for each pair, and a judgement on whether the whole analysis was valid.
  • ▸POST-HOC: a t test for every pair — Mozart vs Michael, Mozart vs Weather, Michael vs Weather.
  • ▸THE PROBLEM: each test risks 5%, so across three the chance of getting all three right is only 0.95³ ≈ 86% — treating them as independent, which is the lecture’s approximation.
  • ▸BONFERRONI: divide alpha by the number of tests. α per test = .05 ⁄ 3 = .0167, and 0.9833³ ≈ 95% — back where you started.
🪝 memory hookF opens the door; post-hoc says which room. And every extra question costs you.

In words

A significant F is a doorway, not a destination. It says the means are not all equal and nothing at all about which — and finding out costs you something. This step is about that cost, and about the conditions the whole method assumed from the start.

  • Every pairwise test uses the pooled sₚ = √(MS Within) on df Within, which is why the ANOVA has to come first.
  • THE THREE ASSUMPTIONS: population scores normally distributed; equal variances in the population; independent observations.

Rules of the game for step 12

  • Post-hoc tests only after a significant F. In this course the omnibus test is the gate.
  • The correction makes each test HARDER, deliberately. That is the price of not fooling yourself.
  • Equal group sizes are NOT an assumption. Unequal n is fine, which is exactly why SS Between weights each group by its own n.

Say it to a friend

“The test said these three aren’t all the same, but it wouldn’t tell me which. So I checked the pairs — and because I was asking three questions instead of one, I had to set a tougher bar for each.”

Key insight

“Not distinguishable” is a perfectly good finding. A significant F with only one separating pair is completely normal, and so is a significant F where no single pair survives the correction.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Running the pairs at an uncorrected .05 — that is what manufactures differences that are not there. And running them at all after a non-significant F.

🧪 Demo 7 — what each extra comparison costs

Check yourself

Five groups. How many pairwise comparisons, and what is the Bonferroni alpha at an overall .05?
Pairs = k(k − 1)/2 = 5 × 4 / 2 = 10. Bonferroni α = .05 ⁄ 10 = .005 per test.
The largest group SD is 7.2 and the smallest is 3.1. Is the equal-variance assumption in trouble?
Yes — the usual rule of thumb asks that the largest sample SD be no more than twice the smallest, and 7.2 is more than 2 × 3.1 = 6.2. Worth flagging.
